title: Summarizing Billions of File Transfers a Month- OpenSearch Transforms for OSDF
---

<p>The Open Science Data Federation (OSDF) now produces about two billion file-transfer records a month, roughly 780 a second so far in 2026 and close to 2,000 a second at peak. That is about 17 times the 2021 rate, 39 times the rate of job records in GRACC, our long-standing job accounting system, and 6 to 11 times the OSPool’s HTCondor accounting records. To keep this data queryable, we summarize it into one row per day for each combination of directory, server, site and user domain with OpenSearch transform jobs. The summaries are more than 8,000 times smaller than the raw records, and questions that took minutes against the raw records now answer in under a second. The price is per-file detail: the summaries can say how much a directory moved on a day, but not which files.</p>
 <h2 id="motivation">Motivation</h2> <p>In 2026 OSDF has recorded 17 times as many file transfers a second as it did in 2021, and most of that growth came in the last 18 months. XRootD monitoring on each cache and origin reports every event of a transfer, such as a file open and a file close, often in separate packets. The events pass through RabbitMQ to the collector, which joins each transfer’s events into one record and stores it as one document in OpenSearch. Each transfer record counted here therefore stands for several messages through RabbitMQ and the collector.</p>

 <p>This is small next to industry log pipelines, but OSDF is now the largest accounting stream our community runs. It outpaces both GRACC and the OSPool’s HTCondor accounting, which ships job, epoch and transfer history from the access points into two Elasticsearch clusters.</p>

Its raw records alone are 9 times the 4.6 billion documents in both accounting clusters, and they take 17.5 TB on disk (35 TB with replicas), more than twice GRACC’s 7.3 TB going back to 2015. RabbitMQ and the collector handle several event messages for each of these records, OpenSearch stores every record, and dashboards that read the raw records slowed to minutes or timed out.</p>
 <p>RabbitMQ carries even more than the records. In September 2026 the collector received an average of 3,700 monitoring packets a second and emitted 1,300 transfer records a second, nearly three packets per record. The <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">xrd-rabbitmq</code> broker as a whole took in 8,000 messages a second and delivered 8,700. On its busiest day of the past three months, August 21, 2026, it averaged 19,000 messages a second in.</p>
 <h2 id="implementation">Implementation</h2> <p>The summaries are built by OpenSearch transform jobs, which aggregate the raw transfer records into one document per day and dimension combination. A transform runs a composite aggregation over its source indices, page by page, and writes each bucket as an ordinary document in a target index.</p>
 <p>Each bucket is one UTC day crossed with seven dimensions: <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">dirname1</code>, <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">dirname2</code>, <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">logical_dirname</code>, <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">server</code>, <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">server_hostname</code>, <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">site</code> and <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">user_domain</code>. For each bucket the transform stores the minimum, maximum, sum, average and count of <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">filesize</code>, <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">read</code>, <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">readv</code> and <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">write</code>, and the number of transfer records it summarizes. Output goes to one index per year, <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">osdf-summary-&lt;year&gt;</code>.</p>

 <p>The jobs are split by time:</p>
 <ul> <li><strong>One one-shot transform per completed month</strong> (<code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">osdf-summary-2026-09</code>). It runs once over its month with 10,000-bucket pages, then stops.</li> <li><strong>One continuous live transform per year</strong> (<code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">osdf-summary-2026-from-10-29</code>), starting on the day it was installed. It runs every hour and recomputes only the buckets that new records touch.</li> <li><strong>A one-shot for the current month’s earlier days</strong>, which ends where the live transform starts.</li> </ul> <h3 id="why-transforms-and-not-rollups">Why transforms and not rollups</h3> <p>We first tried an OpenSearch rollup job with the same dimensions in November 2025. Its output index turned out to be hard to use:</p>
 <ul> <li><strong>No documents, only aggregations.</strong> A rollup index refuses any search that returns documents: “Rollup search must have size explicitly set to 0.”</li> <li><strong>Only the aggregations the job precomputed.</strong> Distinct counts are refused (“The cardinality aggregation is not currently supported in rollups”), and so is a plain min or max of <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">@timestamp</code>.</li> <li><strong>Not mixable.</strong> A search across a rollup index and an ordinary index fails on the rollup side.</li> <li><strong>No date filter.</strong> A rollup walks the whole source history window by window from the beginning. After three days ours was still in February 2020, and it ended in a failed state.</li> </ul> <p>A transform’s output is an ordinary index with plain field names such as <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">read_sum</code>, so Grafana, OpenSearch Dashboards and Python scripts query it like any other index. A transform also takes a <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">data_selection_query</code>, so each month can be built, rebuilt and checked on its own.</p>
 <h3 id="what-we-give-up">What we give up</h3> <p>The summaries keep volumes per directory, server, site and user domain per day. Everything else in a raw record is dropped:</p>

 <h2 id="lessons-learned">Lessons learned</h2> <p>The biggest lesson: a transform’s cost is set by how much data each page scans, so the jobs must be cut small in time and use large pages.</p>
 <ul> <li><strong>Every page re-scans the whole query range.</strong> A transform pages through its buckets with a composite aggregation, and each page searches every document its query matches. Our first transform covered all of 2025 with 1,000-bucket pages. Each page read all 16 billion 2025 records and took 9 to 15 minutes, so it would have needed months to finish.</li> <li><strong>One month per job, 10,000-bucket pages.</strong> With a one-month query, OpenSearch skips most of the 1,100+ <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">xrd-stash*</code> shards before searching, and larger pages mean ten times fewer scans. September to December 2025 was rebuilt in under two hours. October 2026, 1.35 billion records, took 75 minutes in 14 pages.</li> <li><strong>Continuous transforms are slow per row.</strong> A continuous transform writes at most 113 buckets per page, because it re-queries each page with one term clause per bucket and OpenSearch caps those at 1,024 / (groups + 1). Its first pass also walks the source shard by shard, and saves its progress only when a run finishes. A live transform that started four weeks back would have needed days, so ours start on the day they are installed; the October 29 one finished its first pass in under three hours.</li> <li><strong>A stuck transform looks healthy.</strong> A transform that is disabled but still reports status <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">started</code> never runs again and never shows as failed. The old 2025 transform sat like this from May to October 2026. An <a href="https://derekweitzel.com/2026/02/13/opensearch-transform-silent-failure-ghost-key/">earlier post</a> covers another quiet failure: the 2022 transform, which failed with only a generic error.</li> </ul> <h3 id="verification">Verification</h3> <p>We checked the summaries against the raw records in two ways:</p>
 <ol> <li><strong>Totals per month.</strong> For each month, the sum of the summaries’ record counts must equal a <code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">_count</code> of the raw records with the same query. Every 2026 month matched exactly, from 2.01 billion records in January to 3.01 billion in September, and so did October 1–28 (1,347,932,941). The 2024 total matches the raw records to the record as well (3,915,896,868).</li> <li><strong>Random days, per directory.</strong> A script picks 50 days at random from a year. For each day it sums bytes read and counts transfers from the raw records, in total and for each top-level directory (<code class="language-plaintext highlighter-rouge">dirname1</code>), and compares them with the summary rows. For 2022 that was 669 day-and-directory pairs, and every value matched exactly.</li> </ol> <h2 id="evaluation">Evaluation</h2> <p>Questions that take three to four minutes on the raw records answer in under a second from the summaries, which take about 8,400 times less disk.</p>

 <p>The summaries also outlive the raw records. All raw records from 2020 to 2023 have been deleted, but the summaries still hold all 6.6 billion transfers from those years.</p>
 <h2 id="conclusions">Conclusions</h2> <p>Transforms gave us what we needed: complete daily summaries from 2020 to today that match the raw records, take a few gigabytes, answer in under a second, and keep history after the raw records age out.</p>
 <p>The backfill is done, so the system is now in a steady state. The day-to-day work is small.</p>
 <p>For anyone summarizing high-volume logs in OpenSearch: prefer transforms to rollups when people need to query the output freely, keep each job’s time range small, and start continuous transforms close to the present.</p>
 <h2 id="what-is-the-osdf">What is the OSDF?</h2> <p>The <a href="https://osg-htc.org/services/osdf/">Open Science Data Federation</a> (OSDF) is a national data distribution network run by the OSG Consortium. Origins connect an institution’s storage, such as a POSIX filesystem or an S3 or HTTP endpoint, to the federation. Caches hosted by CHTC, ESnet, Internet2, the University of Nebraska, NRP, Syracuse University and PATh keep copies near where jobs run, which cuts wide-area traffic and load on the origins. The OSDF runs software from the Pelican Platform and serves OSPool researchers, collaborations that share datasets, and anyone reading over HTTPS from a browser or Python.</p>
